UX Analysis of a Data Visualisation

During my Fundamentals of AI certificate course, I wrote this UX analysis of an OECD data visualisation.

OECD OURdata Index​

The OECD Open Useful Reusable Data (OURdata) Index is a measure of the quality of open-government data policies and practices across OECD countries. It is made up of three sub-indices: Data Availability, Data Accessibility and Government Support for Data Reuse. Each of these indices makes up one third of a country’s overall OURdata score.

The visualization below uses 2017 and 2019 OURdata scores to indicate the change in effectiveness of open-government policies across the OECD between these years.

Assessment of the Visualisation​

Visual encodings​

For each country, the chart compares 2017 and 2019 OURdata indices. For the 2019 indices, it uses length and colour as the visual encodings, which seems appropriate. Each subcategory contributes proportionately to the length of the stacked bar, and the length of the stacked bar represents the overall OURdata score. The colours that distinguish the subcategories seem well chosen, and do not seem like they would pose a problem for someone with colour blindness. Overall, the 2019 data is clearly represented.

However, the 2017 data is represented in a different way. The visual encodings are shapes to indicate categories, and position along the 2019 bar to indicate how the 2017 scores compare with the 2019 scores.

This difference in visual encodings means that the chart is not readily interpretable. A reader’s visual fixation will initially pick out the bars, and may not even register the presence of the shapes until after some study of the chart.

They will need to study the legend at the top to determine which colour corresponds to which shape. This is particularly necessary for bars such as Denmark’s (DNK), on which the correspondence between colours and shapes is not very discernible from the positions of the shapes.

In addition, the circle shape serves two purposes, as it represents both the Government Support for Reuse index and the overall OURdata index.

It is possible to discern how the 2017 and 2019 scores compare for each country, but it is not the first thing that the user sees. There is strong visual emphasis on the 2019 data. By studying the positions of shapes in relation to the stacked bars, the reader can see roughly how scores have changed, but this requires more focus and cognitive load than should be necessary. For a chart whose title indicates that it is a comparison of 2017 and 2019, it is not well designed.

Data-Ink Ratio​

The chart could benefit from some simplification. The grid contributes little to the clarity of the chart, and could be replaced with a plain white background.

The number of rows means that the horizontal scale at the bottom is far distant from most rows, making it hard to use it as a reference when studying the upper reaches of the chart. However, the chart already gives the overall 2019 score beside each bar, and if this were extended by adding the individual scores to each bar also, there would be no need for any horizontal scale, so this could be dispensed with.

The legend at the top has a grey background. This contributes nothing to clarity, and makes the chart look less clean, so it can be replaced with a white background.

The chart title seems appropriate, and the note at the bottom seems appropriate also, so these could be retained.

Audience​

This chart is intended for any reader interested in Open Government progress, so it needs to be appropriate for people with a broad range of backgrounds. It cannot assume the reader has specialised knowledge, so needs to be as generalised in its presentation as possible. In this regard, although the chart has shortcomings in its clarity, it is probably pitched appropriately. With some study, a general reader can interpret it successfully.

Focus​

The chart has a well-defined focus: comparison of 2017 and 2019 OURdata indices. It does not represent this comparison as well as it should, but the question itself is well chosen.

Chart Type​

A stacked bar chart is a good choice to represent the OURdata indices. Combining this with shapes more suitable for a scatter chart is not a good choice. It would be better to turn it into a clustered stacked chart so that 2017 and 2019 data are both represented in the same way, allowing for direct comparison.

Colour Choice​

The use of a categorical colour palette for the stacked bars is appropriate, as it allows for clear comparison. The palette itself seems appropriate, as there is a clear contrast between the colours, and the colours themselves do not seem like they would be problematic for people with colour blindness.

Redesign 1: Clustered Stacked Bar Chart​

Visual Encodings​

As described above, the original chart combined two different visual encodings to represent the same type of information (2017 and 2019), leading to a confusing picture in which the chart demanded focused attention to discern the answer to the question it focused on.

In this redesign, the 2017 and 2019 data get the same visual encoding. For each country, there is a 2017 and 2019 stacked bar, showing a direct visual comparison of the years.

The subcategories are distinguished by colour.

The individual scores are shown on the bars, and an explanatory subtitle makes clear how the overall score is calculated.

Data-Ink Ratio​

The redesign has no horizontal axis or gridlines because the scores are shown on each bar.

However, a legend is necessary to explain what each colour represents.

The explanatory subtitle adds clarity because it explains how the overall score is calculated, so this use of ink is justified.

Audience​

This chart is intended for any reader interested in Open Government progress, so it needs to be appropriate for people with a broad range of backgrounds. The chart demands no specialised knowledge or advanced interpretation skills. The explanation of the score calculation ensures that any reader knows what the chart is indicating.

Focus​

The chart asks the same question as the original by comparing of 2017 and 2019 OURdata indices per country. In this redesigned version, the question is answered more directly, providing a direct visual comparison for each country.

Chart Type​

The redesign is a clustered stacked bar chart, clustering by year. This provides a direct comparison between 2017 and 2019 for each individual country’s scores. There is a single type of visual encoding to represent each country’s scores per year.

Colour Choice​

The redesigned chart uses a palette that facilitates colour-blind readers. Colours of somewhat contrasting luminance were chosen to be doubly sure, while attempting to avoid differences that might seem to indicate relative importance of different subcategories.

Redesign 2: Map Chart​

This alternative way of presenting the 2017 and 2019 OURdata information changes the focus slightly so that the emphasis is explicitly more on the 2019 index rather than an immediately visible comparison of 2017 and 2019. However, the comparison is still available by hovering over each country. The example above shows the information available when the reader hovers over Canada.

Because the OECD countries are spread across the world, the visualisation is presented as a dashboard with four separate maps showing the regions for which data is available.

Visual Encodings​

Here, the primary visual encoding is colour, using a diverging palette to indicate the 2019 OURdata score for each country. A colour blindness-friendly blue-orange range is used. Blue indicates a higher score, and orange indicates a lower score. One possible drawback of this is that a colour range good at indicating relative scores, but less so at indicating absolute scores.

The map itself might also be considered a visual encoding (shape). It provides a way of directly accessing information relating to a specific country. It is highly amenable to the reader’s visual fixation, because the eye naturally scans from country to country.

Data-Ink Ratio​

The dashboard has an acceptable data-ink ratio. The country categories are immediately clear without the use of text. The title and subtitle give a quick description of the chart and how it functions.

One potential drawback with this approach is the relatively high amount of text in the tooltips, which are used to break down the overall scores into individual scores, and to provide the comparison with 2017 scores.

Audience​

This chart is intended for any reader interested in Open Government progress, so it needs to be appropriate for people with a broad range of backgrounds. The map format and simple tooltip explanations mean that any generic reader should immediately be able to interpret the chart.

Focus​

The chart has a slightly different focus from the original version. Its immediate message is relative 2019 scores across the OECD. However, the question that was answered by the original version is also answered here through the breakdown shown in the tooltips.

Chart Type​

The map format exploits the fact that the original dataset relates directly to geospatial references. The OURdata scores are placed over on the geospatial layer to indicate relative scores per country and provide an interactive way to access the 2017-2019 comparison.

Colour Choice​

The blue-orange diverging palette was chosen to strike a balance between facilitating readers with colour blindness and providing intuitive colour coding to flag countries with good and bad scores. As orange is red-adjacent, it can be intuitively interpreted as a warning signal for low scores.

Interactivity​

This dashboard is interactive. It requires the reader to hover over a country to get the full set of information. As such, it is not ideally suited to a flat presentation in a PDF, PowerPoint, or similar. For this reason, its efficacy is more limited than Redesign 1.
